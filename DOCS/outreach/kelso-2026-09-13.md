Dear Dr Kelso,

I have been keeping a witnessed archive of the public Starlink ephemeris files since 30 August, and
I would rather you saw it before anyone else does.

The problem I started from is that SpaceX publishes about eleven thousand ephemeris files roughly
every eight hours at api.starlink.com/public-files/ephemerides/, and deletes them when the next set
lands. I pull each cycle once using conditional requests, hash every file, build a Merkle root per
cycle, anchor the root in Bitcoin through OpenTimestamps, push the manifest and ten root-selected
files into the Wayback Machine so an independent copy exists that I do not control, and ship the raw
bytes to cold storage. As of 13 September 08:20 UTC that is 44 cycles, all complete, all
Bitcoin-attested, 991 GB as served and 408 GB stored gzipped. These are the operator's predictions,
not observations, and once a cycle is gone from the feed the files are not re-fetchable from the
source, which is the reason for the hashing and the timestamps rather than a preference for them.

On top of that I have started measuring something you will recognise. For each satellite I take the
public element set, propagate it with SGP4, and difference it against the operator's own published
trajectory at the same instants. At the first instant of each operator file, where the prediction
horizon is zero, the public catalogue puts 90 percent of the scored Starlink satellites within 10 km
of where the operator says they are, with a median separation of 2.0 km. By how old the public
element set is, the median separation runs 1.2 km at 0 to 6 hours, 5.3 km at 12 to 24 hours, 13.3 km
at 24 to 48 hours and 32.3 km at 48 to 72 hours. Both sides are predictions and much of the spread
is planned trajectory changes the public set cannot know about, so I am careful not to call it an
error of the satellite or a statement about which side is right. It is what the public catalogue can
see. Figures as of the cycle above; the method and the caveats are on the site.

I am not publishing anything about anyone, and there is no per-operator grading here. Starlink is
simply the only operator with public covariance; anyone else would enter through CelesTrak SupGP
without it.

Three things, and no is a fine answer to all of them.

First, would the archive be useful to you? It is the raw operator files, kept, hashed and timestamped,
with roots anyone can check. If you have a SupGP backfill that predates my first cycle, the two
together would make a far better dataset paper than either alone, and I would rather co-author one
with you than publish a thin one on my own.

Second, if you have five minutes to tell me where the measurement above is wrong or naive, that is
worth more to me now than after it is public.

Third, a small practical note. celestrak.org times out on TCP 443 from my residential IP in Cape Town
and has since 30 August, while space-track.org and api.starlink.com are reachable from the same
machine. It answers normally from a datacentre address, tested 12 September. I mention it in case a
block is broader than intended on a South African ISP range, because it is not something I would work
around.

The site is https://ephemera.space and the code is at github.com/Kira-Ryan/ephemera, MIT. This is a
personal project on a home machine in Cape Town, with no funding and no affiliation to any operator
or agency, and it exists because SpaceX publishes the feed openly at all.
